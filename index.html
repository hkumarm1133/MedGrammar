Patient-Facing Imperative Language in Clinical Plan Documentation

In clinical documentation, especially within the Plan section of SOAP notes, the preferred style is a direct imperative voice. This approach gives clear, concise clinical instructions while maintaining a professional and patient-centered tone.

❓ What Is Patient-Facing Imperative Language?

Patient-facing language refers to documentation written in a manner that is:

  • Clear and easy to understand
  • Respectful and nonjudgmental
  • Action-oriented
  • Appropriate for communication that may be reviewed by patients

Within this broader communication style, the imperative voice is used for plans and follow-up instructions. We need clear, actionable steps — not suggestions or indirect language.

🔑 Key Distinction: Discussion vs. Plan

The most important distinction in clinical documentation:

  • Discussion = Past tense (what happened during the visit)
  • Plan = Direct action verbs (what to do next)

This separation ensures clarity for all readers — clinicians, staff, and patients.

💬 1. Discussion Section (Past Tense)

Summarize education, risks, and benefits discussed during the visit. Do not use "Clinician" or "Doctor."

Preferred verbs:

Reviewed Discussed Educated on Counseled on Instructed on Explained
✅ Correct Discussion Examples

"Risks and benefits were reviewed."

"Discussed treatment options with patient."

"Educated on signs and symptoms of infection."

"Counseled on importance of medication adherence."

📋 2. Orders (Evaluation)

Preferred verbs for orders:

Order Obtain Schedule Arrange Follow up
✅ Correct Order Examples

"Order CBC."

"Obtain MRI of lumbar spine."

"Schedule follow-up in 4 weeks."

✅ 3. Plan Section (Treatment/Actions) — Direct Imperative Tone

The Plan section uses imperative, direct language. One bullet per action. Do NOT use "advise," "recommend," or "encourage" — indirect language sounds optional and creates ambiguity.

Preferred action verbs:

Continue Start Discontinue Increase Decrease Monitor Follow up Refer Consider Avoid Return if

These verbs directly state the intended action without sounding harsh or authoritarian.

✅ Correct Plan Examples

"Continue current antihypertensive medication."

"Start metformin 500 mg daily."

"Monitor blood glucose levels daily."

"Follow up with cardiology in 2 weeks."

"Consider MRI if symptoms worsen."

"Refer to cardiology."

"Return if symptoms worsen."

"Avoid NSAIDs due to renal impairment."

Why these work:

  • Begin with an action verb
  • Clearly state the intended plan
  • Concise and directive without sounding forceful
  • Maintain a neutral and professional tone
📌 Why Imperative Tone Is Preferred in the Plan Section

The Plan section is intended to communicate actionable next steps. Imperative phrasing:

  • Improves clarity and readability
  • Keeps instructions concise
  • Reduces ambiguity regarding the intended action
  • Aligns with standard clinical documentation practices
  • Supports patient-centered communication when records are patient-accessible

The tone should remain professional and direct rather than commanding or suggestive.

🚫 Why No "Advise," "Recommend," or "Encourage"?

Words like "advise," "recommend," and "encourage" create indirect language that sounds optional. Plans need clear, actionable steps — not suggestions.

❌ Avoid — Sounds Optional / Indirect

"Recommend starting aspirin therapy."

"Advise patient to follow up in 2 weeks."

"Encourage smoking cessation."

"It is recommended that labs be obtained."

✅ Preferred — Clear Action

"Start aspirin therapy."

"Follow up in 2 weeks."

"Discuss smoking cessation strategies."

"Order labs."

⚠️ Less Preferred: Non-Imperative / Passive Construction
⚠️ Less Effective

"Current antihypertensive medication should be continued."

"Blood glucose levels should be monitored daily."

"Smoking cessation strategies were discussed."

Why these are less effective:

  • Passive structure makes the plan less direct
  • Longer and less concise
  • Focus shifts away from the action itself
  • Documentation sounds descriptive rather than action-oriented

These may still be grammatically correct, but they do not reflect the preferred imperative style for plan documentation.

❌ Incorrect or Non-Patient-Friendly Tone
❌ Avoid

"The patient must stop smoking immediately." (commanding)

"Patient is noncompliant with medication." (judgmental)

"You failed to follow diet recommendations." (blaming)

✅ Preferred Alternatives

"Discuss smoking cessation strategies."

"Review medication adherence."

"Discuss dietary goals."

Why these should be avoided:

  • Can sound judgmental or punitive
  • May negatively affect patient-centered communication
  • Less appropriate for records patients may access directly
📝 Key Guidance for Documentation Teams

Core rule: Past tense for Discussion + Direct action verbs for Plan.

When drafting clinical documentation:

  1. Discussion: Use past tense — reviewed, discussed, educated on, counseled on, instructed on, explained.
  2. Orders: Use direct verbs — order, obtain, schedule, arrange, follow up.
  3. Plan: Use imperative, direct language. One bullet per action. No "advise/recommend/encourage."
  4. No "Clinician" or "Doctor" in documentation.
  5. Keep instructions concise and clinically clear.
  6. Maintain a respectful, nonjudgmental tone.
  7. Prefer active, directive phrasing over passive constructions.
  8. Use conditional imperative statements when needed:
    "Consider neurology referral if symptoms persist."
    "Increase hydration if dizziness continues."
    "Return if symptoms worsen."

In summary: Patient-facing language is the overall communication goal, while direct imperative voice is the preferred documentation style used to express clinical plans clearly and professionally.
